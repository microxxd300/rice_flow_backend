Models documented in this notebook:

Part 1 — K-Means Clustering of Rice Varieties¶

1.1 Methodology¶

Why K-Means. K-Means is used to group the varieties in RiceFlow's catalog into agro-climatically similar clusters. The cluster label is stored as supplementary metadata on a recommendation result — it does not itself rank varieties (ranking is produced separately by a weighted Rice Suitability Index). K-Means was chosen over hierarchical or density-based clustering because: the variety catalog is small and unlabeled, the desired output is a flat partition rather than a dendrogram, and K-Means centroids are cheap to persist and reuse at inference time (scaler.pkl + kmeans.pkl, loaded once per prediction).

Feature selection. Each variety is represented as a 9-dimensional vector covering the traits that actually differentiate where and how a variety performs: ecosystem, three stress-tolerance ratings (submergence, drought, salinity), average yield potential, maturity duration, its optimal temperature band, and its minimum rainfall requirement. Categorical traits (ecosystem, tolerance levels) are ordinally encoded; all 9 columns are then standardized (zero mean, unit variance) with StandardScaler so that yield (t/ha, small numbers) does not get swamped by rainfall (mm, large numbers) purely due to scale.

Choosing k. Rather than picking a cluster count by inspection, k is swept over [2, 8] (capped at n_samples - 1) and the k that maximizes the silhouette score is kept. Silhouette score measures how well-separated and internally cohesive the resulting clusters are (range −1 to 1; higher is better) and does not require ground-truth labels, which is appropriate here since there is no external "correct" clustering to validate against. The Davies–Bouldin index (lower is better) is reported alongside as a second, independent measure of cluster separation.

# ── Feature matrix — identical encoding to clustering.py::_build_feature_matrix ──
TOLERANCE_MAP = {'low': 0, 'moderate': 1, 'high': 2}
ECOSYSTEM_MAP = {'irrigated_lowland': 0, 'rainfed_lowland': 1, 'upland': 2}

FEATURE_NAMES = [
    'ecosystem', 'submergence_tolerance', 'drought_tolerance', 'salinity_tolerance',
    'avg_yield_t_ha', 'maturity_days', 'optimal_temp_min', 'optimal_temp_max',
    'optimal_rainfall_min_1000mm',
]

def build_feature_matrix(df):
    rows = []
    for _, v in df.iterrows():
        rows.append([
            ECOSYSTEM_MAP.get(v.ecosystem, 0),
            TOLERANCE_MAP.get(v.submergence_tolerance, 0),
            TOLERANCE_MAP.get(v.drought_tolerance, 0),
            TOLERANCE_MAP.get(v.salinity_tolerance, 0),
            v.avg_yield_t_ha,
            v.maturity_days,
            v.optimal_temp_min,
            v.optimal_temp_max,
            v.optimal_rainfall_min / 1000,
        ])
    return np.array(rows, dtype=float)

1.3 Model selection — sweeping k¶

For each candidate k, K-Means is fit with n_init=50 (50 random centroid initializations, keeping the best by inertia) and random_state=42 for reproducibility. Silhouette score and Davies–Bouldin index are recorded for every k so the choice of final k is transparent and auditable, not picked by eye.

In [5]:
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score, davies_bouldin_score

K_MIN, K_MAX = 2, 8
n_samples = X_scaled.shape[0]
k_hi = min(K_MAX, n_samples - 1)

search_log = []
best = None
for k in range(K_MIN, k_hi + 1):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, random_state=42, n_init=50)
    labels = km.fit_predict(X_scaled)
    sil = round(float(silhouette_score(X_scaled, labels)), 4)
    dbi = round(float(davies_bouldin_score(X_scaled, labels)), 4)
    search_log.append({'k': k, 'silhouette': sil, 'davies_bouldin': dbi})
    if best is None or sil > best['silhouette']:
        best = {'model': km, 'labels': labels, 'silhouette': sil, 'davies_bouldin': dbi, 'k': k}

search_df = pd.DataFrame(search_log)

1.6 Limitations¶

  • The catalog currently has 17 varieties; cluster stability should be re-evaluated as more varieties are added to the system.
  • K-Means assumes roughly spherical, similarly-sized clusters in the scaled feature space; if future traits introduce strongly non-convex groupings, a density-based method (e.g. DBSCAN) may fit better.
  • Cluster labels are supplementary metadata only — the actual variety-to-farm ranking in RiceFlow is computed by a separate, deterministic Rice Suitability Index, not by cluster membership.

Part 2 — Linear Regression Yield Prediction¶

2.1 Methodology¶

Why Linear Regression. RiceFlow's yield estimator is used as a decision-support figure shown to farmers and extension staff, so interpretability is prioritized: every input's contribution to the predicted yield can be read directly off its fitted coefficient. A more flexible model (random forest, gradient boosting) could fit the training data more closely, but at this stage — with model inputs and thresholds themselves derived from literature rather than a large labeled farm dataset — a transparent linear model is the more defensible baseline, and it is the model actually deployed in apps/predictions/services.py.

Capturing non-linear agronomy with a linear model. Rice yield does not respond linearly to soil pH, heat, or rainfall — yield peaks at an optimum pH and declines on either side of it, heat stress only bites above a sterility threshold, and both drought and waterlogging separately cut yield in opposite directions. A plain linear model cannot express any of that. The fix used here is feature engineering: six additional columns are computed from the raw inputs so that the engineered columns are what enters the (still linear) model, while jointly they encode the known non-linear relationships:

Engineered feature Formula Captures
ph_stress (pH − 6.2)² distance from the pH optimum (rice grows best near pH 6.2)
heat_stress max(0, temp − 33) spikelet sterility risk once flowering temperature exceeds ~33 °C
rain_deficit max(0, 1400 − rainfall) / 1000 water-limited / drought conditions below ~1400 mm
rain_excess max(0, rainfall − 2600) / 1000 waterlogging / flood loss above ~2600 mm
flood_x_rain flood_risk_encoded × rain_excess flood-prone fields suffer disproportionately more from excess rain
ph_x_om pH × organic matter nutrient-availability interaction

These thresholds (pH 6.2 optimum, ~33 °C heat-sterility onset, ~1400–2600 mm adequate rainfall band) follow documented rice agronomy guidance (IRRI Rice Knowledge Bank / PhilRice production guides), not arbitrary tuning.

2.2 Data source — real vs. synthetic¶

RiceFlow's production code (_get_real_training_data()) always prefers real, farmer-submitted YieldRecord rows once at least 30 exist for a given farm/cycle. Panabo City does not yet have 30 real yield records in the system — the app has been in active use for a limited time — so, exactly as the deployed backend does, this notebook falls back to a synthetic training set generated from the documented agronomic relationships above, with realistic noise added. This is stated explicitly here rather than disguised: the dataset below is a literature-grounded simulation used as an interim baseline until sufficient real farm data accumulates, at which point retraining on real records is a drop-in replacement (same feature pipeline, same code path).

]
FEATURE_NAMES_LR = RAW_FEATURES + ENGINEERED_FEATURES

PH_OPTIMUM     = 6.2
HEAT_THRESHOLD = 33.0
RAIN_LOW       = 1400.0
RAIN_HIGH      = 2600.0

def engineer_features(X_raw: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    '''Must stay identical between training and prediction (mirrors services.py).'''
    ph, om, temp, rain, flood_enc = X_raw[:, 1], X_raw[:, 2], X_raw[:, 3], X_raw[:, 4], X_raw[:, 7]

    ph_stress    = (ph - PH_OPTIMUM) ** 2
    heat_stress  = np.maximum(0.0, temp - HEAT_THRESHOLD)
    rain_deficit = np.maximum(0.0, RAIN_LOW - rain) / 1000.0
    rain_excess  = np.maximum(0.0, rain - RAIN_HIGH) / 1000.0
    flood_x_rain = flood_enc * rain_excess
    ph_x_om      = ph * om

    return np.column_stack([X_raw, ph_stress, heat_stress, rain_deficit, rain_excess, flood_x_rain, ph_x_om])


def generate_training_data(n_samples: int = 5000):
    '''Synthetic dataset following documented rice-agronomy relationships
    (identical formulation to apps/predictions/services.py::_generate_training_data).'''
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

    area_ha        = rng.uniform(0.5, 5.0,   n_samples)
    soil_ph        = rng.uniform(4.5, 8.0,   n_samples)
    organic_matter = rng.uniform(0.5, 4.0,   n_samples)
    avg_temp       = rng.uniform(24, 36,     n_samples)
    rainfall       = rng.uniform(800, 3500,  n_samples)
    humidity       = rng.uniform(60, 90,     n_samples)
    elevation_m    = rng.uniform(0, 600,     n_samples)
    flood_risk_enc = rng.integers(0, 3,      n_samples).astype(float)
    ecosystem_enc  = rng.integers(0, 2,      n_samples).astype(float)
    maturity_days  = rng.uniform(100, 130,   n_samples)
    variety_avg_y  = rng.uniform(4.0, 7.5,   n_samples)

    ph_stress    = (soil_ph - PH_OPTIMUM) ** 2
    heat_stress  = np.maximum(0.0, avg_temp - HEAT_THRESHOLD)
    rain_deficit = np.maximum(0.0, RAIN_LOW - rainfall) / 1000.0
    rain_excess  = np.maximum(0.0, rainfall - RAIN_HIGH) / 1000.0

    yield_t_ha = (
        variety_avg_y * 0.55
        + 1.2 * np.exp(-ph_stress / 0.9)
        + 0.22 * organic_matter
        - 1.5 * rain_deficit
        - 0.5 * rain_excess
        - 0.35 * flood_risk_enc * rain_excess
        - 0.10 * heat_stress ** 1.5
        + 0.20 * (1 - ecosystem_enc)
        - 0.15 * flood_risk_enc
        - elevation_m / 2500.0
        + rng.normal(0, 0.35, n_samples)
    )
    yield_t_ha = np.clip(yield_t_ha, 1.5, 9.5)

    X = np.column_stack([
        area_ha, soil_ph, organic_matter, avg_temp, rainfall, humidity,
        elevation_m, flood_risk_enc, ecosystem_enc, maturity_days, variety_avg_y,
    ])
    return X, yield_t_ha

2.4 Train/test split and cross-validation¶

An 80/20 train/test split is used, with 5-fold cross-validation run on the training split only, to get a stability estimate (mean ± std of R²) rather than relying on a single lucky split.

2.8 Limitations & future work¶

  • Synthetic training data. Until at least 30 real, farmer-submitted yield records exist for the target area (Panabo City, Davao del Norte), the model trains on a literature-grounded simulation rather than observed outcomes. The production code path (_get_real_training_data()) switches to real records automatically and transparently once that threshold is met — no code change is required, only data accumulation through normal app usage.
  • Linear model ceiling. Even with engineered non-linear features, a linear model cannot capture interaction effects that were not explicitly engineered. If/when a larger real dataset is available, a non-linear model (e.g. gradient boosting) could be compared against this baseline.
  • Geographic specificity. The agronomic thresholds used (pH 6.2 optimum, 33 °C heat threshold, 1400–2600 mm rainfall band) are general rice-agronomy values, not empirically fit to Panabo City specifically — they should be revisited once local yield data is available.

Conclusion¶

Two complementary models support RiceFlow's recommendation pipeline:

  • K-Means (Part 1) groups the 17-variety catalog into agro-climatically coherent clusters (best k selected by silhouette score), used as supplementary metadata alongside the system's primary, rule-based suitability ranking.
  • Linear Regression (Part 2) estimates expected yield from farm/soil/ climate conditions, using engineered features to express known non-linear agronomic stress responses within an interpretable linear model, trained on a literature-grounded synthetic dataset pending sufficient real local yield records.

Both models are trained with fixed random seeds for full reproducibility, and both persist to the same artifact format (joblib .pkl) loaded by the deployed Django backend at inference time.
